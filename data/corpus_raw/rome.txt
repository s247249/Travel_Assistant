 === FILE: destination_rome.md ===

 id: destination_rome
 type: destination
 city: Rome
 country: Italy
 tags: ["culture", "history", "touristy", "city"]
 budget_level: medium
 best_for: [family, couples, young, foodies]

 Overview

 Rome is Italy's capital and its most overwhelming open-air museum: three thousand years of history layered into a single walkable city. The Colosseum and Roman Forum anchor the ancient core, Vatican City holds Michelangelo and St Peter's across the river, and between them
 lie baroque piazzas, Bernini fountains, medieval lanes and neighbourhoods — Trastevere, Monti, Testaccio — where daily Roman life continues around the monuments. This is a culture-and-history city first and foremost, dense, loud, grand and imperfect, with a food culture
 serious enough to justify a trip on its own.

 Vibe & who it suits

 Choose Rome if you want immersion rather than repose. Compared with Italy's coastal destinations, Rome offers no beaches or slow seaside rhythm; its equivalent of a lazy afternoon is a park bench in Villa Borghese or a long lunch in Testaccio. It is genuinely touristy —
 queues, crowds and souvenir stalls are part of the deal — but rewards those who book ahead and wander early or late. Families find endless storytelling material, couples get cinematic evenings, young travellers find nightlife and street energy, and foodies discover
 carbonara, supplì and artisan gelato at the source. Travellers whose primary goal is swimming, spa resort idleness or empty landscapes should weigh a seaside or countryside Italian base instead, perhaps with Rome as a two- or three-day add-on. Budget expectations sit at a
 medium level: entry fees and dining add up, but many of the city's greatest pleasures — piazzas, churches, hilltop sunsets — are free or inexpensive.

 When to go

 For the June–September 2026 window, expect real heat and real crowds. June brings long, warm evenings and manageable mornings; July and August are hot and humid, with August the peak for prices and domestic holiday crowds even as some local businesses take their own breaks.
 September eases the temperature while keeping the long light. Plan indoor museums and classes for midday, monuments for early morning, and hills and riverside walks for evening.

 Getting around

 Rome's historic centre is best on foot, with the metro's limited lines and a dense bus and tram network filling the longer gaps. Two airports serve the city, both connected to the centre by rail or coach. Within the core, expect cobblestones, hills and pedestrian zones —
 comfortable shoes matter more than any transport pass.

 === FILE: guide_rome.md ===

 id: guide_rome
 type: guide
 city: Rome
 country: Italy
 topics: [transport, food, seasonality, day-planning]

 Transport

 Rome is served by two airports: Fiumicino, the main international hub, linked to the centre by a direct express train to Termini station plus regional trains and coaches; and Ciampino, used mainly by low-cost carriers, connected by bus and rail combinations. Termini is the
 city's rail and metro crossroads. The metro has only a few lines and skirts rather than penetrates the archaeological core, so buses, trams and walking do the real work. Validate tickets and expect surface traffic to be slow at rush hours. The historic centre is compact
 enough to cross on foot in under an hour, and walking is genuinely the best way to see Rome — just budget for cobblestones, sampietrini, and the occasional hill. Taxis and ride apps are useful late at night; use official ranks rather than street hails.

 Food

 Roman cuisine is proud and specific: pasta classics like carbonara, cacio e pepe, gricia and amatriciana; fried supplì rice balls; thin pizza al taglio sold by weight; seasonal artichokes; and the offal-rich tradition of Testaccio for the curious. Start the day standing at a
 bar with a cappuccino and cornetto, lunch simply, and dine later than northern Europeans expect — Roman evenings start late. Neighbourhood choice matters: Trastevere for atmosphere, Testaccio for tradition, Monti for wine bars. House wine in trattorias is usually decent and
 inexpensive; tourist-menu restaurants with picture boards near monuments are best avoided. Gelato quality varies wildly — look for muted, natural colours rather than fluorescent piles.

 Day-planning tips

 Group sights geographically: ancient Rome (Colosseum, Forum, Palatine) fills one morning-to-early-afternoon block; the Vatican Museums and St Peter's deserve another; the baroque centre — Pantheon, Piazza Navona, Campo de' Fiori, Trevi — makes a walkable third. Book timed
 entries for the Colosseum, Vatican Museums and Borghese Gallery well ahead, then build the rest of the day around those fixed anchors. Families should plan one major sight per day plus a park or gelato reward, and lean on hands-on activities like cooking classes. Young
 travellers can push days longer, adding evening food tours, sidecar rides or the Janiculum sunset. Everyone should resist the urge to see everything: Rome rewards return visits.

 Seasonality

 From June to September 2026, Rome runs hot and busy. June offers the best balance of long evenings and tolerable mornings. July and August bring strong heat and humidity, with August the peak month for prices and crowds — book accommodation and major entries as early as
 possible, and note that some family-run shops and restaurants close for their own holidays around mid-August. September softens the heat while crowds persist. In all summer months, structure days around the sun: monuments at opening, air-conditioned museums and classes at
 midday, a proper rest, then piazzas, hills and riverside evenings. Carry water — the city's free drinking fountains, nasoni, are everywhere — and dress for church entry requirements: covered shoulders and knees.
