## Transport

Rome is served by two airports: Fiumicino, the main international hub, linked to the centre by a direct express train to Termini station plus regional trains and coaches; and Ciampino, used mainly by low-cost carriers, connected by bus and rail combinations. Termini is the city's rail and metro crossroads. The metro has only a few lines and skirts rather than penetrates the archaeological core, so buses, trams and walking do the real work. Validate tickets and expect surface traffic to be slow at rush hours. The historic centre is compact enough to cross on foot in under an hour, and walking is genuinely the best way to see Rome — just budget for cobblestones, sampietrini, and the occasional hill. Taxis and ride apps are useful late at night; use official ranks rather than street hails.

## Food

Roman cuisine is proud and specific: pasta classics like carbonara, cacio e pepe, gricia and amatriciana; fried supplì rice balls; thin pizza al taglio sold by weight; seasonal artichokes; and the offal-rich tradition of Testaccio for the curious. Start the day standing at a bar with a cappuccino and cornetto, lunch simply, and dine later than northern Europeans expect — Roman evenings start late. Neighbourhood choice matters: Trastevere for atmosphere, Testaccio for tradition, Monti for wine bars. House wine in trattorias is usually decent and inexpensive; tourist-menu restaurants with picture boards near monuments are best avoided. Gelato quality varies wildly — look for muted, natural colours rather than fluorescent piles.

## Day-planning tips

Group sights geographically: ancient Rome (Colosseum, Forum, Palatine) fills one morning-to-early-afternoon block; the Vatican Museums and St Peter's deserve another; the baroque centre — Pantheon, Piazza Navona, Campo de' Fiori, Trevi — makes a walkable third. Book timed entries for the Colosseum, Vatican Museums and Borghese Gallery well ahead, then build the rest of the day around those fixed anchors. Families should plan one major sight per day plus a park or gelato reward, and lean on hands-on activities like cooking classes. Young travellers can push days longer, adding evening food tours, sidecar rides or the Janiculum sunset. Everyone should resist the urge to see everything: Rome rewards return visits.

## Seasonality

From June to September 2026, Rome runs hot and busy. June offers the best balance of long evenings and tolerable mornings. July and August bring strong heat and humidity, with August the peak month for prices and crowds — book accommodation and major entries as early as possible, and note that some family-run shops and restaurants close for their own holidays around mid-August. September softens the heat while crowds persist. In all summer months, structure days around the sun: monuments at opening, air-conditioned museums and classes at midday, a proper rest, then piazzas, hills and riverside evenings. Carry water — the city's free drinking fountains, nasoni, are everywhere — and dress for church entry requirements: covered shoulders and knees.
