## Overview

Rome is Italy's capital and its most overwhelming open-air museum: three thousand years of history layered into a single walkable city. The Colosseum and Roman Forum anchor the ancient core, Vatican City holds Michelangelo and St Peter's across the river, and between them lie baroque piazzas, Bernini fountains, medieval lanes and neighbourhoods — Trastevere, Monti, Testaccio — where daily Roman life continues around the monuments. This is a culture-and-history city first and foremost, dense, loud, grand and imperfect, with a food culture serious enough to justify a trip on its own.

## Vibe & who it suits

Choose Rome if you want immersion rather than repose. Compared with Italy's coastal destinations, Rome offers no beaches or slow seaside rhythm; its equivalent of a lazy afternoon is a park bench in Villa Borghese or a long lunch in Testaccio. It is genuinely touristy — queues, crowds and souvenir stalls are part of the deal — but rewards those who book ahead and wander early or late. Families find endless storytelling material, couples get cinematic evenings, young travellers find nightlife and street energy, and foodies discover carbonara, supplì and artisan gelato at the source. Travellers whose primary goal is swimming, spa resort idleness or empty landscapes should weigh a seaside or countryside Italian base instead, perhaps with Rome as a two- or three-day add-on. Budget expectations sit at a medium level: entry fees and dining add up, but many of the city's greatest pleasures — piazzas, churches, hilltop sunsets — are free or inexpensive.

## When to go

For the June–September 2026 window, expect real heat and real crowds. June brings long, warm evenings and manageable mornings; July and August are hot and humid, with August the peak for prices and domestic holiday crowds even as some local businesses take their own breaks. September eases the temperature while keeping the long light. Plan indoor museums and classes for midday, monuments for early morning, and hills and riverside walks for evening.

## Getting around

Rome's historic centre is best on foot, with the metro's limited lines and a dense bus and tram network filling the longer gaps. Two airports serve the city, both connected to the centre by rail or coach. Within the core, expect cobblestones, hills and pedestrian zones — comfortable shoes matter more than any transport pass.
