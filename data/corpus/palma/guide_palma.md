## Transport
Palma de Mallorca is easy to navigate without a car. A direct public bus links the airport, just east of the city, with the central intermodal station in a short, inexpensive ride; taxis wait outside arrivals for door-to-door transfers. There is no need for a metro: the historic core of Palma de Mallorca is compact and walkable, and the flat seafront promenade connects the marina, old town edges and beach districts. City buses run along the bay to Cala Major, the port and Playa de Palma, while the intermodal hub near Plaza España links rail and bus lines for island trips. Cycling suits the seafront lanes. For the Tramuntana and Formentor, guided excursions beat summer self-driving.

## Food
Eating well is central to Palma de Mallorca. Look for sobrasada, the soft spreadable cured sausage; ensaïmada, the island's spiral pastry; pan amb oli, bread rubbed with ramallet tomatoes and olive oil; tumbet, layered vegetables; and fresh fish and rice dishes. Mallorcan wines from Binissalem and Pla i Llevant deserve a place at the table. Santa Catalina is the dining quarter of Palma de Mallorca, with a lively market hall by day and tapas bars from old-school to inventive by night. Lunch is the main meal and runs late; dinner waits for the evening cool. Costs are moderate, with seafront terraces trending premium.

## Day-planning tips
Structure days in Palma de Mallorca around the heat: culture in the morning, water or shade in the afternoon, food and neighbourhoods after sunset. Group sights geographically — cathedral, patios and Es Baluard form one walkable cluster; Bellver pairs with its forest; Santa Catalina anchors meals. Book popular entries, small-group tours and boat trips ahead in summer. Families should plan one anchor activity daily plus beach time, with shallow Playa de Palma or the hotel pool as fallback; young travellers can pair a morning hike or e-bike ride with an evening sail. Keep one unscheduled half-day for drifting.

## Seasonality
From June to September 2026, Palma de Mallorca is in full summer. June balances warm swimmable sea, long evenings and pre-peak prices. July brings steady heat and growing family crowds. August is the peak — hottest, most crowded and most expensive; reserve early, start at dawn, and lean on air-conditioned museums and shaded patios at midday. September softens: the sea stays warm, crowds and prices ease, and evening life remains lively. Whenever you visit Palma de Mallorca, pack serious sun protection, plan hydration into every outing, and treat the midday hours as the sea's, not the city's.
