## Overview
Paris, the capital of France, is one of the world's great cultural cities: a dense, walkable capital where royal palaces, world-class museums and grand boulevards layer two thousand years of history into a single skyline. The Louvre and the Musee d'Orsay anchor an art scene of unmatched depth, the Eiffel Tower and Sacre-Coeur define the views, and neighbourhoods like the Marais, Montmartre and the Latin Quarter each preserve a distinct character. Beyond the icons, Paris rewards slow travel: market streets, covered passages, garden chairs by fountains, and a food culture that runs from corner bakeries to celebrated dining rooms. Luxury is part of the city's identity, in its fashion houses, palace hotels and refined service traditions.

## Vibe & who it suits
Paris is unapologetically cultural and touristy, and honest planning matters: major sights draw crowds, and the budget level is high compared with much of France. The city suits couples seeking romance and atmosphere, foodies chasing cheese, wine and patisserie, and seniors who appreciate museums, cafes and elegant pacing. Young travellers find nightlife and local canal-side life; families do well with gardens and structured visits. Travellers whose priority is sea, swimming or pure beach relaxation should look to France's coastal destinations instead; Paris offers culture, not coastline.

## When to go
For June to September 2026, Paris delivers long daylight and warm weather, with July and August the hottest stretch; heatwaves can make midday sightseeing draining, so plan indoor museums for peak afternoon heat. August is the crowds-and-prices peak for visitors even as some Parisians leave town, and certain small shops and restaurants close for their own holidays. June and September are generally more comfortable, with softer light and slightly calmer queues. Whatever the month, book major entries and popular experiences well ahead.

## Getting around
Paris is best explored by metro and on foot. The metro network is dense and reaches nearly every sight, buses fill the gaps and show the city above ground, and regional trains handle day trips such as Versailles. Many itineraries work entirely on foot within central districts. Airports connect to the centre by rail and road links. Walking between grouped sights is often faster and far more rewarding than crossing the city repeatedly, so structure days by neighbourhood.
