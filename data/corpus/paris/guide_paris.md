## Transport
Getting around Paris is straightforward once you accept two principles: the metro goes almost everywhere, and walking is part of the experience. Both major airports connect to central Paris by rail links and by road, so arrival transfers are simple to plan without exact timetables; allow a generous buffer after landing. Within the city, the metro's dense network puts most sights within a short ride plus a walk, and buses offer a scenic above-ground alternative on the same corridors. Regional trains reach Versailles and other day-trip destinations from central stations. Buy a pass or ticket book suited to your length of stay rather than fumbling per journey, keep tickets handy for gates and checks, and remember that many central hops, such as Louvre to Marais or Latin Quarter to Luxembourg, are pleasanter on foot than underground.

## Food
Paris eating spans every budget mood, even in a high-cost city. Bakeries supply breakfast and picnic lunches cheaply and superbly; baguettes, croissants and quiches are daily fuel. Bistro classics include onion soup, steak-frites, duck confit and seasonal salads, while cheese counters and wine shops turn any park bench into a feast. The Marais and Latin Quarter concentrate casual eating, Montmartre leans touristy but atmospheric, and market streets reward grazing. Sit-down meals follow French rhythms: lunch is a real pause, dinner starts later than many visitors expect, and popular restaurants merit reservations. Tap water and bread come with meals; tipping is a modest courtesy, not an obligation. For families, picnics and crepes are reliable wins; foodies should reserve at least one serious tasting or dining experience.

## Day-planning tips
Structure Paris days by neighbourhood, not by checklist. Group nearby sights, such as the Louvre with the Tuileries and Palais-Royal, or Luxembourg Gardens with the Latin Quarter and Pantheon, to avoid zigzagging across the city. Book timed entries for headline attractions well ahead, and slot them for opening-time mornings or late afternoons to dodge peak crowds. Families should plan one major visit per day plus a park release valve, with picnic lunches to keep costs and tantrums down. Young travellers can pack evenings with canal picnics, cabaret or nightlife after lighter daytime pacing. Seniors and relax-seekers do best with a museum morning, a long cafe lunch and a garden afternoon. Build in unplanned wandering; Paris rewards it more than most cities.

## Seasonality
For June through September 2026, expect long days and warm to hot weather in Paris. June offers pleasant temperatures and manageable crowds; July heats up; August is the peak for visitor crowds and prices, with heatwaves possible and some local businesses on their own summer closure, even as tourist infrastructure runs fully. September brings relief: milder air, golden light and thinner queues at major sights. Across the whole window, book popular entries, cruises and day trips early, carry water and sun protection, and use air-conditioned museums or shaded gardens during the hottest hours. Evenings stay light late, which makes Seine-side strolls, canal picnics and terrace dinners the natural reward after a day of culture.
